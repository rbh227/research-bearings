DA-SegFormer: Domain-Adaptive Transformers for Building Damage
Assessment from Very High Resolution Satellite Imagery

Jane Q. Researcher        Alex B. Coauthor        Sam C. Lastauthor
Department of Computer Science, Example University
{jqr, abc, scl}@example.edu

Abstract

Rapid building damage assessment after a natural disaster depends on
pairs of pre- and post-event satellite images that are rarely drawn from
the same sensor, season, or geography as the data a model was trained
on. We present DA-SegFormer, a domain-adaptive segmentation transformer
that aligns pre- and post-event features through a shared attention
bottleneck and an adversarial domain discriminator over event types. On
the xBD benchmark DA-SegFormer reaches 0.79 harmonic mean F1 over the
four damage classes, and on a held-out wildfire subset never seen in
training it reaches 0.61, against 0.44 for the strongest published
baseline. We show that most of the gain comes from the alignment term
rather than the backbone, and we release code and trained weights.

1. Introduction

Building damage assessment is the task of labelling every building
footprint in a post-event image with a damage grade, typically on the
four-level scale introduced with the xBD dataset: no damage, minor
damage, major damage, and destroyed. The task matters because the first
72 hours after a disaster are when allocation decisions are made, and
those decisions are currently made from windshield surveys and helicopter
footage.

The dominant formulation treats this as semantic segmentation over a
stacked pre- and post-event image pair. A U-Net or a ResNet-FPN encoder
consumes the six-channel stack and predicts a per-pixel damage grade,
which is then pooled over the footprint polygon. This formulation is
what the xView2 challenge established, and nearly every subsequent
published system inherits it.

We argue that the formulation hides the problem that actually limits
deployment. Models trained on xBD generalize poorly to events whose
disaster type is not represented in training, and wildfire is the
clearest case: xBD contains six wildfire events out of nineteen, all in
California, all from one sensor family. A model that has memorized what
California chaparral looks like after a burn does not transfer to
Australian eucalyptus.

This paper makes three contributions. First, we characterize the
transfer gap quantitatively across disaster types, showing a mean drop
of 0.31 F1 when the event type is held out. Second, we propose
DA-SegFormer, which adds a domain discriminator over event type and a
shared attention bottleneck between the pre- and post-event streams.
Third, we show through ablation that the alignment term, not the
transformer backbone, is responsible for most of the improvement, which
contradicts the prevailing assumption that architecture is the lever.

2. Related Work

Damage assessment from remote sensing has a long history predating deep
learning, beginning with change-detection methods over multispectral
imagery. The xBD dataset and the accompanying xView2 challenge moved the
field onto a common benchmark and a common formulation.

Domain adaptation in remote sensing has largely followed the general
computer vision literature, with adversarial feature alignment and
self-training as the two dominant families.

3. Method

DA-SegFormer builds on the SegFormer backbone, replacing the decoder
with a two-stream design. The pre-event and post-event streams share
weights in the first three stages and diverge thereafter.

The domain discriminator is a three-layer MLP over the pooled bottleneck
representation, trained with a gradient reversal layer at coefficient
0.1, warmed up linearly over the first ten epochs.

The discriminator is trained jointly with the segmentation head using a
weighted sum of cross-entropy over damage grades and the reversed domain
loss. We weight the four damage classes inversely to their frequency in
the training split, because destroyed buildings are roughly one twentieth
as common as undamaged ones and an unweighted loss collapses to the
majority class within two epochs.

Training uses AdamW with a cosine schedule, a base learning rate of 6e-5,
a batch size of 16 image tiles of 512 by 512 pixels, and 120 epochs on
eight A100 GPUs. Augmentation is limited to flips, ninety-degree
rotations, and per-channel brightness jitter; we deliberately exclude
elastic deformation and cutout, which in preliminary runs degraded the
minor-damage class.

3.1 Shared attention bottleneck

The pre-event and post-event streams are encoded independently through
the first three SegFormer stages. At stage four we concatenate the two
token sequences and apply four transformer blocks with full attention
across both, so that a token in the post-event image can attend to the
corresponding region before the event. This is the mechanism by which
the model sees change rather than appearance.

We tried three alternatives before settling on this one: early fusion by
channel stacking, which is what the U-Net baselines do; late fusion by
feature differencing; and cross-attention with the pre-event stream as
keys and values only. Full attention across both outperformed all three
by between 0.02 and 0.05 harmonic mean F1.

3.2 Domain discriminator

The domain label is the disaster type: earthquake, flood, hurricane,
tsunami, volcano, or wildfire. The discriminator predicts this label from
the pooled bottleneck, and the gradient reversal layer means the encoder
is trained to make the label unpredictable.

We use disaster type rather than geography as the domain label because
type is what the deployment gap is organized around: a model is
typically asked about a disaster of a kind it has seen, in a place it has
not, or the reverse. Geography as the label gave a smaller and less
consistent gain in early runs.

4. Experiments

4.1 Data and splits

xBD contains 22,068 images across nineteen events, with 850,736 building
polygons annotated on the four-level damage scale. We use the standard
train, hold, and test split released with the dataset for the in-domain
numbers.

For the transfer numbers we construct leave-one-type-out splits: all
events of one disaster type are removed from training and used as the
test set. This gives six transfer settings. We report the wildfire
setting in the main text and the remaining five in the appendix.

We note one property of the standard split that bears on every number
published on this benchmark: the split is by image tile, not by event,
and adjacent tiles from the same event appear in both train and test.
Tiles overlap by 128 pixels in the released tiling. A model can therefore
see part of a test building during training.

4.2 Baselines

We compare against the xView2 challenge winner, a ResNet-34 U-Net
ensemble; against a single-model U-Net reimplementation; against
SegFormer-B2 with channel stacking; and against a recent
change-detection transformer adapted to the four-class output.

We reproduced every baseline ourselves rather than quoting published
numbers, because the published numbers use varying polygon-pooling rules
and are not comparable as printed. Our reproductions land within 0.02 of
the published figures in four cases out of five; the fifth is 0.06 below
and we say so in the appendix rather than excluding it.

4.3 Metrics

We report the harmonic mean of per-class F1 over the four damage grades,
which is the xView2 challenge metric, and we additionally report per-class
F1 because the harmonic mean is dominated by the rarest class.

All numbers are the mean of five seeds with the standard deviation in
parentheses. We report seed variance because in our own early runs the
difference between two seeds of the same configuration exceeded the
difference between two configurations, and single-seed comparisons on
this benchmark are therefore not informative.

5. Results

5.1 In-domain

On the standard split DA-SegFormer reaches 0.79 (0.01) harmonic mean F1
against 0.77 (0.01) for the strongest baseline. This difference is small
and we do not claim it as the contribution.

5.2 Transfer

On the held-out wildfire split DA-SegFormer reaches 0.61 (0.03) against
0.44 (0.05) for the strongest baseline. The gap is largest on the
major-damage class, which is where the visual signature differs most
between fire and the other disaster types.

5.3 Ablation

Removing the domain discriminator and keeping the backbone drops the
transfer number from 0.61 to 0.48. Replacing the SegFormer backbone with
a ResNet-FPN and keeping the discriminator gives 0.58. The alignment term
is therefore responsible for most of the gain, and the backbone for
little of it.

This is the result we consider the paper's contribution, and it
contradicts the prevailing assumption in this literature that the
architecture is the lever worth pulling.

6. Discussion

The obvious limitation is that our transfer evaluation still trains and
tests on xBD, which is one sensor family, one annotation protocol, and
one vendor's imagery. A genuinely out-of-distribution test would use a
different sensor, and we do not have labelled data for one.

The second limitation is the tile-overlap property of the standard split
described in section 4.1. It inflates every in-domain number in this
literature including ours. Our transfer numbers are not affected, because
the held-out type shares no event with training.

7. Conclusion

We characterized the disaster-type transfer gap on xBD, proposed a
domain-adaptive segmentation transformer that narrows it, and showed by
ablation that the alignment term rather than the backbone is what
narrows it.
